Can't climb the hill? Climb a floor that sits under it, again and again. That's EM.

Bound optimization swaps a hard objective for an easy lower bound that touches it at your current guess. Climbing the bound can only lift the real objective. EM is the version for models with hidden variables: E guesses the hidden parts, M refits the model as if those guesses were data. §8.7.1

The general algorithm: climb a floor that touches the hill

We want to maximize some function \(\ell(\theta)\), usually a log-likelihood. MM (minorize-maximize) builds a surrogate \(Q(\theta,\theta^t)\) that sits under \(\ell\) everywhere and touches it at the current guess \(\theta^t\). Then it jumps to the top of the surrogate and repeats.

Explain itAt your level

You're trying to get to the top of a hill, but it's so foggy you can only feel the ground right under your feet.

You have a magic tent. When you set it up, it always fits under the ground, and its pole touches the ground exactly where you're standing. The tent is a simple dome, so its top is easy to find.

Walk to where the tent is tallest. The tent is under the ground everywhere, so the ground at that spot is at least as high as the tent there. And the tent's top is at least as high as where you started. So you're never lower than before.

Set the tent up again at your new spot and repeat. Step by step you get higher, and you never slide back down.

Picture the objective \(\ell(\theta)\) as a curvy graph with no easy formula for its peak. At your current guess \(\theta^t\), draw a downward parabola that:

  1. passes through the point \((\theta^t, \ell(\theta^t))\), and
  2. stays below the curve everywhere else.

You can find a parabola's peak with the vertex formula \(x = -b/2a\). Call that peak \(\theta^{t+1}\). Then three facts chain together:

  • The curve is above the parabola at \(\theta^{t+1}\) (the parabola is always under it).
  • The parabola's peak is at least as high as the parabola at \(\theta^t\) (it's the peak).
  • The parabola at \(\theta^t\) equals the curve at \(\theta^t\) (they touch).

So \(\ell(\theta^{t+1}) \ge \ell(\theta^t)\). The score can't go down. In the Play box, try shrinking the parabola's curvature until it pokes above the curve: the guarantee breaks.

\(Q\) minorizes \(\ell\) at \(\theta^t\) if \(Q(\theta,\theta^t)\le \ell(\theta)\) for all \(\theta\) (lower bound) and \(Q(\theta^t,\theta^t)=\ell(\theta^t)\) (tight). The update \(\theta^{t+1}=\arg\max_\theta Q(\theta,\theta^t)\) then gives the monotone chain in Eq. 8.145.

The demo's surrogate comes from Taylor's theorem. If \(|\ell''|\le L\) everywhere, then \(\ell(\theta)\ge \ell(\theta^t)+\ell'(\theta^t)(\theta-\theta^t)-\tfrac L2(\theta-\theta^t)^2\). That quadratic is a valid minorizer, and its argmax is \(\theta^t+\ell'(\theta^t)/L\): gradient ascent with step size \(1/L\).

Newton vs MM. Newton uses the actual curvature \(\ell''(\theta^t)\). That's a better local fit, but it's an approximation, not a bound. Where \(\ell\) curves upward (\(\ell''>0\)), Newton's "parabola" opens upward and its stationary point is a minimum. Where curvature is small, Newton leaps too far. Both can lower the objective (Figure 8.24).

Debugging rule: if your MM/EM objective ever decreases, there is a bug in your math or code.

MM generalizes EM, IRLS-with-curvature-bounds (Böhning–Lindsay's quadratic lower bound, e.g. \(-\tfrac14X^\top X\) for logistic regression), iterative scaling, NMF multiplicative updates and many proximal schemes. The descent lemma for \(L\)-smooth \(\ell\) is the canonical quadratic minorizer; gradient ascent with step \(1/L\) is MM.

  • Convergence. Monotonicity plus boundedness gives convergence of \(\ell(\theta^t)\). Under continuity of the map \(M(\theta^t)=\arg\max Q(\cdot,\theta^t)\) (Zangwill / Wu 1983 conditions), limit points are stationary points of \(\ell\). They could be saddles, though that's rare in practice.
  • Rate. Typically linear, with rate set by the spectral radius of \(DM\) at the fixed point, roughly \(I-(\nabla^2 Q)^{-1}\nabla^2\ell\). A tight bound gives fast convergence and a loose bound gives slow convergence. For EM, that ratio is the fraction of missing information.
  • Acceleration (SQUAREM, Anderson, momentum, quasi-Newton on \(M\)) can be dramatically faster but gives up monotonicity unless safeguarded.
  • Practice. Assert \(\ell(\theta^{t+1})\ge\ell(\theta^t)-\varepsilon\) in your training loop. It catches gradient-sign errors, stale sufficient statistics and off-by-one normalizers in one line.

Math, slowlyEquations 8.144 – 8.145

The two rules a surrogate must obey

\[ \underbrace{\class{tb}{Q(\theta,\theta^t)} \le \class{to}{\ell(\theta)} \text{ for every } \theta}_{\text{lower bound}} \qquad\qquad \underbrace{\class{tb}{Q(\theta^t,\theta^t)} = \class{to}{\ell(\theta^t)}}_{\text{touches at the current guess}} \]

If both hold we say \(Q\) minorizes \(\ell\). The update is just "go to the top of the surrogate":

\[ \theta^{t+1} = \arg\max_\theta \class{tb}{Q(\theta,\theta^t)} \tag{8.144} \]

Why the objective can never go down

\[ \class{to}{\ell(\theta^{t+1})} \;\overset{①}{\ge}\; \class{tb}{Q(\theta^{t+1},\theta^t)} \;\overset{②}{\ge}\; \class{tb}{Q(\theta^t,\theta^t)} \;\overset{③}{=}\; \class{to}{\ell(\theta^t)} \tag{8.145} \]
  1. ① lower bound. \(Q(\cdot,\theta^t)\) is under \(\ell\) at every point, so in particular at \(\theta^{t+1}\). In the plot, the vertical green gap between the square and the dot above it.
  2. ② argmax. \(\theta^{t+1}\) is the best point on \(Q\), so it beats every other point, including \(\theta^t\). You can't climb a parabola and end up lower than where you started on it.
  3. ③ tightness. The surrogate touches \(\ell\) at \(\theta^t\). This is what ties the bound's progress back to the real objective.
Drop rule ① (curvature below 1× in the demo) and the chain breaks at the first link. That is exactly what you see when the shaded "pokes through" region contains \(\theta^{t+1}\).

The quadratic bound used in the demo

\[ \class{tb}{Q(\theta,\theta^t)} = \ell(\theta^t) + \ell'(\theta^t)(\theta-\theta^t) - \frac{c\,L}{2}(\theta-\theta^t)^2, \qquad L=\max_\theta|\ell''(\theta)| \]

Setting \(\partial Q/\partial\theta = 0\) gives \(\theta^{t+1}=\theta^t+\ell'(\theta^t)/(cL)\). Newton instead uses \(\theta^{t+1}=\theta^t-\ell'(\theta^t)/\ell''(\theta^t)\), the stationary point of an approximation that has no reason to stay under \(\ell\).

§8.7.2

The EM algorithm: a chicken-and-egg problem, solved by taking turns

Each example has visible data \(y_n\) and hidden data \(z_n\), such as which cluster a point came from. If we knew the \(z_n\), fitting \(\theta\) would be easy. If we knew \(\theta\), guessing the \(z_n\) would be easy. EM alternates between the two, and it turns out to be an MM algorithm, so it never makes the likelihood worse.

Explain itAt your level

Two bakeries, Rosie's and Blue Door, both sell cookies. You find a big pile of cookies, all mixed up, with no labels.

You want to know what each bakery's "usual cookie" is like. If you knew who baked each cookie, that would be easy: sort them into two piles and look at each pile. If you knew what each bakery's usual cookie was like, sorting would be easy too: put each cookie with the bakery it looks most like.

You know neither! So you take turns:

  1. Guess who made each cookie, using your current idea of each bakery. If you're not sure, say "70% Rosie's, 30% Blue Door."
  2. Update your idea of each bakery using those guesses.
  3. Go back to step 1.

Each round your story about the cookies fits a little better, and it never gets worse.

EM is two steps on repeat:

  • E step (Expectation). Using the current model, compute the probability of each hidden label for each data point, for example "point 7 is 82% cluster 1." This uses Bayes' rule.
  • M step (Maximization). Treat those probabilities as fractional counts and refit the model. Point 7 counts as 0.82 of a point for cluster 1 and 0.18 for cluster 2. The new cluster centers are weighted averages.

Why does this work? The E step builds a floor under the log-likelihood that touches it at the current model. The M step climbs that floor. That's the MM picture from §8.7.1, so the log-likelihood can only go up.

The observed-data log-likelihood \(\ell(\theta)=\sum_n\log\sum_{z_n}p(y_n,z_n\mid\theta)\) has a log of a sum, so it doesn't decouple. EM:

  • E step: \(q_n^t(z_n)=p(z_n\mid y_n,\theta^t)\). This makes the ELBO tight at \(\theta^t\).
  • M step: \(\theta^{t+1}=\arg\max_\theta \sum_n \mathbb E_{q_n^t}[\log p(y_n,z_n\mid\theta)]\). This is a complete-data MLE with soft labels, usually in closed form.

Defining \(Q(\theta,\theta^t)=\text{ELBO}(\theta,\{q^t_n\})\) gives a minorizer, so EM inherits Eq. 8.145. It converges to a local maximum (more precisely, a stationary point). How fast depends on how much information is missing.

EM is coordinate ascent on \(\mathcal{L}(\theta,q)\) (Neal & Hinton 1998): the E step maximizes over \(q\) (KL projection), the M step over \(\theta\). This view justifies partial E steps (incremental / online / sparse EM) and partial M steps (GEM, ECM) without losing monotonicity.

  • Rate. Dempster–Laird–Rubin: the Jacobian of the EM map at \(\hat\theta\) is \(I_c^{-1}I_m\), where \(I_m = I_c - I_o\) is the missing information. When clusters overlap heavily, \(I_m\approx I_c\) and EM crawls.
  • Fisher's identity. \(\nabla\ell(\theta^t)=\nabla_\theta Q(\theta,\theta^t)\big|_{\theta=\theta^t}\), so the E-step statistics also give exact gradients if you'd rather use L-BFGS on \(\ell\) directly.
  • When the posterior is intractable: variational EM (non-tight bound), Monte Carlo EM, stochastic EM. With an amortized encoder and SGD, this becomes the VAE.

Math, slowlyThe plan

Notation

  • \(y_n\): what we see for example \(n\) (a geyser measurement).
  • \(z_n\): what we don't see (which cluster it came from).
  • \(\theta\): the parameters (cluster means, covariances, weights).

The loop

  1. E step: for each \(n\), compute \(q_n(z_n) = p(z_n\mid y_n,\theta^t)\). Fill in the hidden data, but as probabilities, not hard guesses.
  2. M step: \(\theta^{t+1}=\arg\max_\theta \sum_n\sum_{z_n} q_n(z_n)\log p(y_n,z_n\mid\theta)\). Fit the model as if the filled-in data were real, weighting each guess by its probability.
  3. Repeat. The guesses depend on θ and θ depends on the guesses, so one pass isn't enough.

The next three sections answer three questions in turn. Why is there a lower bound at all? (§8.7.2.1) Why does the E step make it touch? (§8.7.2.2) Why is the M step easy? (§8.7.2.3)

§8.7.2.1

The lower bound: Jensen's inequality turns a log-of-sum into a sum-of-logs

The log-likelihood has a \(\log\) wrapped around a \(\sum_{z}\), and that's what makes it hard. The trick is to insert a distribution \(q_n(z_n)\) and push the log inside the sum. You pay for it by getting a lower bound instead of an equality.

Explain itAt your level

"Log" is a squisher. It squishes big numbers a lot and small numbers only a little.

Take two numbers, like 1 and 9. Average first, then squish: you squish 5. Squish first, then average: you average a squished 1 and a squished 9. Because the 9 got squished extra hard, the second way always comes out smaller.

So "squish-then-average" makes a floor under "average-then-squish." The thing we really want is the second one, but it's hard to work with. The floor is easy to work with, and if we push the floor up, the real thing has to be at least that high too.

We need \(\log(\text{a sum})\), which is messy. Two moves fix that:

  1. Multiply by 1 in disguise. Write each term \(p\) as \(q\cdot\frac{p}{q}\), where the \(q\)'s are any probabilities that add to 1. Now the sum is a weighted average of the ratios \(p/q\).
  2. Jensen's inequality. The log graph bends downward, so the chord between two points on it lies under the curve. That means \(\log(\text{average}) \ge \text{average}(\log)\).

The right side is a sum of logs, and that's easy to maximize. In the Play box, the amber gap is how much we lose. When every ratio \(p/q\) is the same number, the gap is zero.

For any distribution \(q_n\) over \(z_n\):

\(\log\sum_z p(y,z\mid\theta) = \log \mathbb E_{q}\!\left[\frac{p(y,z\mid\theta)}{q(z)}\right] \ge \mathbb E_q\!\left[\log\frac{p(y,z\mid\theta)}{q(z)}\right]\)

because \(\log\) is concave. Split the right side into \(\mathbb E_q[\log p(y,z\mid\theta)] + \mathbb H(q)\). That's the ELBO, the evidence lower bound. It holds for every \(q\), and equality needs \(p(y,z\mid\theta)/q(z)\) to be constant in \(z\). That forces \(q(z)\propto p(y,z\mid\theta)\), i.e. \(q = p(z\mid y,\theta)\). The next section shows this directly.

\(\mathcal L(\theta,q)=\mathbb E_q[\log p(y,z\mid\theta)]+\mathbb H(q)=-F(\theta,q)\), the negative variational free energy. Key identity: \(\log p(y\mid\theta)=\mathcal L(\theta,q)+D_{\mathbb{KL}}(q\,\|\,p(z\mid y,\theta))\).

  • The bound is jointly a function of \((\theta, q)\). EM fixes the family of \(q\) to be unrestricted, so the bound can be tight. Variational inference restricts \(q\) (mean-field, Gaussian, normalizing flow), and the gap becomes a persistent bias.
  • Bounds like IWAE use \(\log\frac1K\sum_k w_k\) with \(K\) importance samples. They are tighter than the ELBO and converge to \(\log p(y)\) as \(K\to\infty\).
  • The ELBO decomposes over \(n\) because the \(q_n\) are independent. That's why EM parallelizes, and why stochastic/online EM works on minibatches.

Math, slowlyEquations 8.146 – 8.150

The objective, and why it's awkward

\[ \class{to}{\ell(\theta)} = \sum_{n=1}^N \log p(y_n\mid\theta) = \sum_{n=1}^N \log\Big[\sum_{z_n} p(y_n,z_n\mid\theta)\Big] \tag{8.146} \]

The hidden \(z_n\) has to be summed out ("marginalized"), so we get a log of a sum. You can't split \(\log(A+B)\) into separate pieces, so the parameters of different clusters stay tangled together.

Multiply by one

\[ \class{to}{\ell(\theta)} = \sum_n \log\Big[\sum_{z_n} \class{tm}{q_n(z_n)}\,\frac{p(y_n,z_n\mid\theta)}{\class{tm}{q_n(z_n)}}\Big] \tag{8.147} \]

Nothing has changed: \(q/q = 1\). But the inside is now an average of the ratios \(p/q\), weighted by \(q\).

Push the log through the average

\[ \class{to}{\ell(\theta)} \;\ge\; \sum_n\sum_{z_n} \class{tm}{q_n(z_n)}\log\frac{p(y_n,z_n\mid\theta)}{\class{tm}{q_n(z_n)}} \tag{8.148} \]
Refresher · Jensen's inequality
For a concave \(f\) (like \(\log\)): \(f(\mathbb E[X]) \ge \mathbb E[f(X)]\). The chord between two points on a concave curve lies under the curve. Equality holds when \(X\) is constant.

Name the two pieces

\[ = \sum_n \Big( \underbrace{\mathbb E_{q_n}\big[\log p(y_n,z_n\mid\theta)\big]}_{\text{expected complete-data log-lik.}} + \underbrace{\mathbb H(q_n)}_{\text{entropy of } q_n} \Big) = \sum_n \class{tb}{Ł(\theta,q_n)} \tag{8.149–8.150} \]
  1. \(\log\frac{p}{q} = \log p - \log q\), so the sum splits in two. Linearity of expectation.
  2. \(-\sum_z q(z)\log q(z)\) is the entropy \(\mathbb H(q)\). It doesn't involve θ at all, which matters for the M step.
  3. The result is the ELBO, the evidence lower bound. The "evidence" is \(\log p(y_{1:N}\mid\theta)\). This same quantity drives variational inference (§4.6.8.3).

§8.7.2.2

E step: choose q to close the gap

The ELBO holds for any \(q\). Which \(q\) makes it best? Rewriting it shows the answer: the gap between the ELBO and the true log-likelihood is a KL divergence, and that's zero exactly when \(q\) is the posterior \(p(z\mid y,\theta)\).

Explain itAt your level

This step is where you make your best guess about the hidden stuff.

Say a cookie is very chocolatey. Rosie's cookies are usually very chocolatey and Blue Door's usually aren't, so you'd guess "probably Rosie's, maybe 80%." If the cookie were halfway in between, you'd say "50/50."

The "floor" from before has a gap under the real answer, and the gap is a measure of how wrong your guesses are. When your guesses are exactly as sure as they should be, the gap disappears and the floor touches the real answer.

For one data point \(y\), the E step asks: what's the probability it came from cluster 1 vs cluster 2? Bayes' rule says

\(P(\text{cluster }1\mid y)=\dfrac{\text{prior}_1\times\text{likelihood}_1}{\text{prior}_1\times\text{likelihood}_1+\text{prior}_2\times\text{likelihood}_2}\).

In the Play box, the slider \(q\) is your guess for cluster 1. The ELBO curve peaks, and touches the true log-probability line, exactly at the Bayes answer. Any other guess leaves an amber gap. That gap is called the KL divergence, a "distance" between your guess and the right answer that is never negative.

Use \(p(y,z\mid\theta)=p(z\mid y,\theta)\,p(y\mid\theta)\) inside the ELBO for one example:

\(\text{Ł}(\theta,q)=\sum_z q(z)\log\frac{p(z\mid y,\theta)}{q(z)}+\log p(y\mid\theta)=-D_{\mathbb{KL}}\big(q\,\|\,p(z\mid y,\theta)\big)+\log p(y\mid\theta)\).

The second term doesn't depend on \(q\). The KL term is \(\ge 0\) and equals 0 iff \(q=p(z\mid y,\theta)\). So the maximizing \(q\) is the posterior, and the bound is then tight: \(\text{Ł}(\theta^t,\{q^*_n\})=\ell(\theta^t)\). Setting \(Q(\theta,\theta^t)=\text{Ł}(\theta,\{p(z_n\mid y_n,\theta^t)\})\) gives exactly the minorizer MM needs.

The E step is the I-projection of the posterior onto an unrestricted family, which is just the posterior. After the M step moves to \(\theta\ne\theta^t\), the gap is \(\sum_n D_{\mathbb{KL}}\big(p(z_n\mid y_n,\theta^t)\,\|\,p(z_n\mid y_n,\theta)\big)\). This explains slow convergence: when the posterior barely changes with \(\theta\), the bound is nearly tight everywhere but also nearly flat in the "missing" directions.

  • The E step only needs whatever statistics the M step consumes. For exponential families, that's \(\sum_n\mathbb E_{q_n}[T(y_n,z_n)]\).
  • Intractable posterior (HMM with huge state space, factorial models, LDA): replace \(q\) by a mean-field or structured approximation. That's variational EM, and the bound is no longer tight, so monotonicity holds for the ELBO, not for \(\ell\). Sampling gives MCEM/SAEM.
  • Numerics: compute \(r_{nk}\) with log-sum-exp. Underflow in \(\pi_kp(y_n\mid\theta_k)\) is the most common EM bug in high dimensions.

Math, slowlyEquations 8.151 – 8.156

Rewrite one term of the ELBO

\[ \class{tb}{Ł(\theta,q_n)} = \sum_{z_n} q_n(z_n)\log\frac{p(y_n,z_n\mid\theta)}{q_n(z_n)} \tag{8.151} \]
  1. \(\displaystyle = \sum_{z_n} q_n(z_n)\log\frac{p(z_n\mid y_n,\theta)\,p(y_n\mid\theta)}{q_n(z_n)}\) (8.152) The product rule: joint = posterior × evidence.
  2. \(\displaystyle = \sum_{z_n} q_n(z_n)\log\frac{p(z_n\mid y_n,\theta)}{q_n(z_n)} + \sum_{z_n} q_n(z_n)\log p(y_n\mid\theta)\) (8.153) Log of a product is a sum of logs.
  3. \(\displaystyle = -\class{ta}{D_{\mathbb{KL}}\big(q_n(z_n)\,\|\,p(z_n\mid y_n,\theta)\big)} + \class{to}{\log p(y_n\mid\theta)}\) (8.154) The first sum is minus a KL divergence. In the second, \(\log p(y_n\mid\theta)\) has no \(z_n\) in it and \(\sum q_n = 1\).
\[ \class{to}{\log p(y_n\mid\theta)} = \class{tb}{Ł(\theta,q_n)} + \class{ta}{D_{\mathbb{KL}}\big(q_n\,\|\,p(z_n\mid y_n,\theta)\big)} \]

The true value equals the bound plus the gap. Because \(D_{\mathbb{KL}}\ge0\) with equality iff the two distributions match, the best choice is

\[ q_n^*(z_n) = p(z_n\mid y_n,\theta) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad Ł(\theta,\{q_n^*\}) = \sum_n \log p(y_n\mid\theta) = \class{to}{\ell(\theta)} \tag{8.155} \]

Connecting to MM

\[ \class{tb}{Q(\theta,\theta^t)} = Ł\big(\theta, \{p(z_n\mid y_n,\theta^t)\}\big) \tag{8.156} \]

Freeze the \(q\)'s at the posterior computed with the old parameters \(\theta^t\), and view the ELBO as a function of the new \(\theta\). It's tight at \(\theta=\theta^t\) and a lower bound everywhere else, so it's a minorizer. If you can only approximate the posterior, the bound isn't tight, and that's variational EM.

§8.7.2.3

M step: fit the model with soft, fractional labels

With the \(q\)'s frozen, the entropy term is a constant. What's left, the expected complete-data log-likelihood, is a weighted version of an ordinary fully observed MLE problem, and for exponential families it has a closed-form answer.

Explain itAt your level

Now pretend your guesses are true, and fix up your idea of each bakery.

If a cookie is "80% Rosie's," it counts as 0.8 of a cookie in Rosie's pile and 0.2 of a cookie in Blue Door's pile. Then you find the middle of each pile, like balancing a seesaw: heavier cookies pull the balance point toward them.

That balance point is your new idea of "Rosie's usual cookie."

Each point \(y_n\) has a weight \(r_{nk}\) for cluster \(k\), its probability from the E step. The new center of cluster \(k\) is the weighted average:

\(\mu_k = \dfrac{r_{1k}y_1 + r_{2k}y_2 + \dots}{r_{1k}+r_{2k}+\dots}\)

The new spread is the weighted average of squared distances from that center, and the new mixing weight \(\pi_k\) is the cluster's share of the total weight. In the Play box, drag μ and watch the score curve. Its peak sits exactly at the weighted average, the "balance point."

The M step objective is \(\ell^t(\theta)=\sum_n\mathbb E_{q_n^t}[\log p(y_n,z_n\mid\theta)]\). We dropped \(\mathbb H(q_n^t)\), which doesn't depend on θ. Because \(\log\) now sits inside the expectation, the joint factorizes and each cluster's parameters separate. Setting \(\partial/\partial\mu_k=0\) for a Gaussian gives the weighted mean.

For an exponential family \(p(y,z\mid\theta)\propto\exp(T(y,z)^\top\theta - A(\theta))\), the objective is \(\sum_n\mathbb E[T(y_n,z_n)]^\top\theta - NA(\theta)\). Setting the gradient to zero gives \(\nabla A(\theta)=\frac1N\sum_n\mathbb E[T]\). That's moment matching on the expected sufficient statistics. So the E step only needs to hand over \(\sum_n\mathbb E[T(y_n,z_n)]\), not every posterior.

Only improvement is needed for monotonicity: \(Q(\theta^{t+1},\theta^t)\ge Q(\theta^t,\theta^t)\). That gives generalized EM (one gradient step), ECM (conditional maximizations over parameter blocks, e.g. means then covariances), and ECME.

  • MAP: add \(\log p(\theta)\) to the M step objective. The E step is unchanged, and conjugate priors keep it closed-form (this is how the collapsing-variance problem in §8.7.3.4 is fixed).
  • Sufficient-statistic form is what makes stochastic/online EM work: keep a running average \(\bar s \leftarrow (1-\rho_t)\bar s + \rho_t\,\mathbb E[T]\) and apply the M step map to \(\bar s\) (Cappé & Moulines 2009).
  • Louis' identity recovers the observed information (standard errors) from complete-data quantities: \(I_o = I_c - I_m\).

Drop what doesn't depend on θ

\[ Ł(\theta,\{q_n^t\}) = \sum_n \mathbb E_{q_n^t}\big[\log p(y_n,z_n\mid\theta)\big] + \underbrace{\sum_n \mathbb H(q_n^t)}_{\text{constant in }\theta} \]
\[ \ell^t(\theta) = \sum_n \mathbb E_{q_n^t(z_n)}\big[\log p(y_n,z_n\mid\theta)\big] \tag{8.157} \]

This is the expected complete-data log-likelihood. "Complete data" means we act as if \(z_n\) were observed, and "expected" means we average over our uncertainty about it.

Exponential family: it all comes down to averages

\[ \ell^t(\theta) = \sum_n \Big(\class{tm}{\mathbb E\big[T(y_n,z_n)\big]}^{\!\top}\theta - A(\theta)\Big) \tag{8.158} \]
  1. Exponential family: \(\log p(y,z\mid\theta) = T(y,z)^\top\theta - A(\theta) + \text{const}\). \(T\) is the sufficient statistic, \(A\) the log-partition function.
  2. Expectation is linear and \(\theta\) is not random, so \(\mathbb E\) passes through to hit only \(T\). These \(\mathbb E[T]\) are the expected sufficient statistics.
  3. Gradient \(=\sum_n \mathbb E[T] - N\nabla A(\theta) = 0\), so \(\nabla A(\theta) = \tfrac1N\sum_n\mathbb E[T]\). Since \(\nabla A(\theta)=\mathbb E_\theta[T]\), the model's expected statistics must match the data's expected statistics: moment matching.
\[ \theta^{t+1} = \arg\max_\theta \sum_n \mathbb E_{q_n^t}\big[\log p(y_n,z_n\mid\theta)\big] \tag{8.159} \]
That's why the table in the Play box only needs three running sums per cluster, \(\sum_n r_{nk}\), \(\sum_n r_{nk}y_n\) and \(\sum_n r_{nk}y_n^2\), to produce \(\pi_k\), \(\mu_k\) and \(\sigma_k^2\). The E step can hand over just those sums.

§8.7.3

Example: EM for a Gaussian mixture on Old Faithful

272 eruptions of the Old Faithful geyser: eruption length (x) against waiting time until the next eruption (y), both standardized. We fit two 2-D Gaussians, starting exactly where the book does: \(\mu_1=(-1,1)\), \(\mu_2=(1,-1)\), \(\Sigma_1=\Sigma_2=I\).

Explain itAt your level

Old Faithful is a geyser that shoots water into the air over and over.

Sometimes it does a short burst and then rests a short time. Sometimes it does a long burst and then rests a long time. Each dot on the picture is one eruption. We want the computer to find the two kinds by itself.

We start with two "blobs" in silly places. Then we repeat: color each dot red or blue (or purple if unsure) by which blob it fits better, then move and stretch each blob to cover its colored dots. Press "Run" and watch the blobs find the two groups.

Each Gaussian blob has a center (mean), a shape (covariance: how wide, how tilted) and a share \(\pi_k\) of the data.

  • E step: for every dot, compute "how likely is this dot under blob 1 × blob 1's share" and the same for blob 2, then divide each by the total. That gives the dot's red/blue color mix.
  • M step: each blob's new center is the color-weighted average of all dots, its new shape is the color-weighted spread, and its new share is its total color divided by 272.

Watch the bottom chart: the log-likelihood only ever goes up. Then turn on the "bug" and see it go down. That's how you'd catch a mistake in your own code.

E step (8.160): \(r_{nk}=\pi_k\mathcal N(y_n\mid\mu_k,\Sigma_k)\big/\sum_{k'}\pi_{k'}\mathcal N(y_n\mid\mu_{k'},\Sigma_{k'})\). M step (8.165–8.167): weighted mean, weighted scatter, and \(\pi_k=r_k/N\) where \(r_k=\sum_n r_{nk}\).

Each M step is a weighted MVN MLE because the expected complete-data log-likelihood (8.164) splits into one weighted Gaussian term per cluster plus a multinomial term for \(\pi\).

Relation to K-means: fix \(\Sigma_k=\sigma^2I\), \(\pi_k=1/K\), and let \(\sigma\to0\). The responsibilities become hard 0/1 assignments to the nearest mean, and EM becomes K-means.

  • Cost per iteration: \(O(NKD^2 + KD^3)\). Use Cholesky factors and log-sum-exp for \(r_{nk}\). Diagonal or tied covariances trade flexibility for stability.
  • Initialization matters: the likelihood is multimodal (\(\ge K!\) symmetric modes from label switching, plus genuinely different local optima). Use K-means++ seeding and multiple restarts.
  • Degeneracy: the MLE is unbounded (a component can collapse onto one point, \(\sigma\to0\)). In practice, use the MAP/NIW prior from §8.7.3.4 or a covariance floor.
  • Slow phases: the long plateau before iteration ~30 here is the classic overlapping-cluster regime. The EM map's Jacobian has eigenvalues near 1 (high missing-information fraction). Acceleration or a few Newton/L-BFGS steps on \(\ell\) help.
  • Standardizing the data (done here) evens out the curvature across dimensions and noticeably speeds convergence.

E step: responsibilities

\[ r_{nk}^{(t)} = p(z_n = k\mid y_n,\theta^{(t)}) = \frac{\pi_k^{(t)}\, p(y_n\mid\theta_k^{(t)})}{\sum_{k'}\pi_{k'}^{(t)}\, p(y_n\mid\theta_{k'}^{(t)})} \tag{8.160} \]

Bayes' rule with prior \(\pi_k\) and likelihood \(\mathcal N(y_n\mid\mu_k,\Sigma_k)\). The hover box in the Play panel shows these numbers for any dot.

M step objective

Write \(z_n\) one-hot: \(z_{nk}=\mathbb I(z_n=k)\). Then \(p(z_n\mid\pi)=\prod_k\pi_k^{z_{nk}}\) and \(p(y_n\mid z_n,\theta)=\prod_k\mathcal N(y_n\mid\mu_k,\Sigma_k)^{z_{nk}}\). The one-hot exponent "selects" the right cluster.

  1. \(\displaystyle \ell^t(\theta)=\mathbb E\Big[\sum_n\log\prod_k\pi_k^{z_{nk}}+\sum_n\log\prod_k\mathcal N(y_n\mid\mu_k,\Sigma_k)^{z_{nk}}\Big]\) (8.161–8.162) Joint = prior on z × likelihood of y given z.
  2. \(\displaystyle =\sum_n\sum_k \mathbb E[z_{nk}]\log\pi_k+\sum_n\sum_k\mathbb E[z_{nk}]\log\mathcal N(y_n\mid\mu_k,\Sigma_k)\) (8.163) Log turns the product into a sum, the exponent comes down, and expectation hits only \(z_{nk}\).
  3. \(\displaystyle =\sum_n\sum_k r_{nk}\log\pi_k-\tfrac12\sum_n\sum_k r_{nk}\big[\log|\Sigma_k|+(y_n-\mu_k)^\top\Sigma_k^{-1}(y_n-\mu_k)\big]+\text{const}\) (8.164) \(\mathbb E[z_{nk}]=r_{nk}\), and we expand the Gaussian log-density.

Each cluster now has its own separate weighted-Gaussian problem.

M step solutions

\[ \class{t1}{\mu_k^{(t+1)}} = \frac{\sum_n r_{nk}^{(t)}\,y_n}{r_k^{(t)}} \tag{8.165} \]
\[ \Sigma_k^{(t+1)} = \frac{\sum_n r_{nk}^{(t)}\,(y_n-\mu_k^{(t+1)})(y_n-\mu_k^{(t+1)})^\top}{r_k^{(t)}} = \frac{\sum_n r_{nk}^{(t)}\,y_ny_n^\top}{r_k^{(t)}} - \mu_k^{(t+1)}(\mu_k^{(t+1)})^\top \tag{8.166} \]
\[ \pi_k^{(t+1)} = \frac1N\sum_n r_{nk}^{(t)} = \frac{r_k^{(t)}}{N}, \qquad r_k^{(t)} \triangleq \sum_n r_{nk}^{(t)} \tag{8.167} \]
  • \(r_k\) is the "effective number of points" in cluster \(k\), a soft count.
  • The mean is the soft-weighted average and the covariance is the soft-weighted scatter. Set \(\partial/\partial\mu_k=0\) and you get \(\sum_n r_{nk}\Sigma_k^{-1}(y_n-\mu_k)=0\).
  • For \(\pi\), maximize \(\sum_k r_k\log\pi_k\) subject to \(\sum_k\pi_k=1\) (Lagrange multiplier). This is the categorical MLE with soft counts.
The "bug" toggle divides the scatter by \(2r_k\), so every M step shrinks the covariances to half the correct value. That's no longer the argmax of \(Q\), so the chain in Eq. 8.145 breaks at link ②, and the trace shows drops.
